--- EXAMPLE 1 ---
Can plain rules replace an AI score? On 8 real resumes they agreed 6 times out of 8, so I kept both and made each show its evidence.

The scores come from Career Pilot, the job-search platform I built and use every day. I built it because the tools I tried over two years of searching were unfinished or failed. Its weakest part was the match score: one AI number out of 100 that never said which skills matched and which were missing.

So I wrote a rule-based score next to it, also out of 100: 60 points for skills, 20 for the role type, 20 for years of experience. For every skill a posting asks for, it shows where that skill appears in the resume: in a job, in a project, or only in the skills list.

The rule that matters most is a limit. A frontend developer posting that also lists Python, SQL, dbt and AWS shares 4 of its 8 skills with a data engineering resume, enough to score 50. Because the role type doesn't match, the score is held at 40, and the AI score gets the same limit.

Then the test, on 8 resumes I had tailored. The two scores were 16.9 points apart on average and agreed on whether a resume needed another rewrite 5 times out of 8. One fix, counting "Tableau or Power BI" as one requirement instead of two, brought that to 12 points and 6 out of 8. Not enough to drop the AI.

So every posting now shows both scores, each skill with the resume line behind it, and a job of the wrong type can't score above 40 under either one.

If you've had rules and a model scoring the same thing, how did you decide when the rules were good enough to replace it?

--- EXAMPLE 2 ---
An auditor checking a ledger in order opens 1,108 entries to find 90% of the problems. Sorted by my risk score, they open 43.

The ledger is simulated. I generated the entries and planted 47 problems, listed in a separate table that the detection code never reads, so every result can be checked against the right answers.

Each entry gets three checks. Benford's Law: in many real financial datasets about 30% of amounts start with 1, so amounts that break that pattern score higher. An Isolation Forest, a model that flags amounts and tax ratios unlike the rest. And a tax-rate rule that flags any claim whose rate doesn't match its category. The pipeline runs in Dagster, so each step can be rerun on its own.

Scoring each check on its own changed how I read the results. The plain tax-rate rule found 45 of the 47 problems and flagged nothing wrongly. The model found 18, and the rule had already found all 18.

So the rule's 45 flags go first, and every one is a real problem: that alone gets an auditor to 90% in 43 entries. The model and Benford's Law rank the rest, and the 2 problems the rule missed come up at entries 131 and 137 of 1,200.

On a real ledger I would start the same way: measure the simple rules alone first, then use a model to rank what they leave.

How do you get labels for a real ledger when nobody plants the answers?
